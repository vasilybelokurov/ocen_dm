    1. Executive summary¶

    • I found no published oMEGACat-specific artificial-star calibration giving absolute completeness at F625W = 19–21 in the central few arcseconds. The catalogue paper’s completeness comparisons establish improved depth, but do not supply the recovery fractions needed to correct your bins. Häberle et al. 2024, oMEGACat II.
    • A marginal central underdensity is documented. Anderson & van der Marel explicitly report fewer stars near the centre than around 10″, after completeness correction. Their companion paper finds the innermost counts compatible with a flat core. This is not an established physical depletion of bright stars. Anderson & van der Marel 2010; van der Marel & Anderson 2010, §4.
    • The weight of resolved-star evidence favours a flat or very shallow projected density profile over a securely detected cusp. The historical cusp claim depended on a centre displaced by approximately 12″ and on unresolved-light measurements. Modern fast-star evidence and a central velocity-dispersion rise concern kinematics, not a newly established number-density cusp. Noyola et al. 2008; Häberle et al. 2025.
    • An IMBH does not require an observable Bahcall–Wolf cusp in the bright-star counts. Relaxation, stellar masses, cusp extent, projection, and the DF determine the prediction; recent omega Cen simulations produce a stellar density slope near 1.3. González Prieto et al. 2025, Appendix A.3.
    • Your counts challenge the specified \(4\times10^4\,M_\odot\) DF more strongly than the no-BH model. Explaining the discrepancy entirely through missing stars would require approximately 43% and 54% completeness in the two bins. The literature does not establish incompleteness this severe for this bright selection.

    2. Detailed findings¶

    Completeness: what has actually been published?¶

    For the HST oMEGACat catalogue, a quantitative central recovery calibration remains unverified. In oMEGACat II, §VII.1 compares magnitude histograms and radial source densities between catalogues. I found no artificial-star experiment, tabulated \(C(m,R)\), or prescription specifically validating core number-density profiles. The official collaboration page lists the catalogue and subsequent releases, but supplies no such calibration itself.

    There are relevant cautions:

    • KS2 cannot measure saturated stars; the team supplements its measurements with hst1pass photometry.
    • Bright stars can have only one short-exposure measurement in F435W or F625W, so requiring multiple measurements can remove otherwise useful stars.
    • Astrometric precision worsens below approximately F625W = 17.5 as saturation affects more exposures. This is not a statement that those stars disappear from the catalogue.
    • The example photometric selection rejects the lowest QFIT decile in magnitude bins and requires \(o<0.5\). These are quality selections, not completeness corrections. Häberle et al. 2024, §§VI.6, IX.2.

    The commonly quoted Nitschai completeness numbers refer to MUSE spectroscopy relative to an HST parent catalogue:

    Source Verified result Applicability
    Nitschai et al. 2023, oMEGACat I For GO spectroscopy with S/N \(>10\), overall completeness reaches 50% at F625W = 18.86; fainter-star completeness is poorer centrally. Spectral extraction completeness, not HST detection completeness.
    Nitschai et al. 2024, oMEGACat III Selecting F625W \(<17\) gives \(>80\%\) completeness in all fields and approximately 90% in most. Supports their bright-RGB population analysis; does not certify F625W \(<19\) HST counts at 2–3″.

    These are measured selection/recovery fractions, not confidence limits. Nitschai et al. 2023, §III.2; Nitschai et al. 2024, Appendix A.

    Earlier HST artificial-star tests do exist. Anderson & van der Marel ran 500,000 tests, including over 700 within 1″ and over 51,000 within 10″. One central \(15″\times15″\) example recovered 3,402/3,847 = 88%; their profile bins had \(>80\%\) completeness even centrally. These results use their older reduction and F435W instrumental selections; they cannot be assigned directly to your oMEGACat F625W bins. Anderson & van der Marel 2010, §§3.5, 5.1.

    A useful 2026 addition is Chen et al.’s JWST artificial-source testing in a central \(2″\times2″\) box: 56,000 injections per NIRCam filter and 42,000 per MIRI filter. Their 50%-recovery magnitude differs by approximately two magnitudes between favourable and unfavourable positions. This demonstrates strong local crowding variation, but calibrates JWST infrared detections, not oMEGACat F625W completeness. Chen et al. 2026, §IV.

    What do the central density profiles show?¶

    Here distinguish [ n(r)\propto r^{-\gamma},\qquad \Sigma(R)\ \text{or}\ I(R)\propto R^{-\Gamma}. ] The historical observed slopes below are projected slopes.

    Noyola, Gebhardt & Bergmann 2008: a shallow light cusp. Their ACS F435W integrated-light profile rises inward with [ \Gamma=0.08\pm0.03. ] The paper reports this slope uncertainty without specifying its confidence convention in the accompanying paragraph. Their adopted centre came from sector-based star counts. Spherical isotropic modelling gave [ M_\bullet=4.0^{+0.75}{-1.0}\times10^4\,M\odot ] at 68% confidence, excluding zero BH at \(>99\%\) under those assumptions. Thus both the photometric cusp and mass inference were conditional on their centre and measurement method. Noyola et al. 2008, §§2, 4 and Fig. 5.

    Anderson & van der Marel 2010: flat counts, with a marginal depression. Their completeness-corrected profiles show no sharp central rise. They write that the

    “density in the centermost bins appears smaller (at marginal significance)”

    than around 10″. Their adopted star-count centre is [ \alpha=13^{\rm h}26^{\rm m}47.24^{\rm s},\quad \delta=-47^\circ28′46.45″, ] with approximately 1″ uncertainty per coordinate, independently supported by kinematics. The reconstructed Noyola centre is 12.3″ away. They attribute the apparent unresolved-light enhancement largely to bright-star PSF wings and shot noise. Anderson & van der Marel 2010, §§4–5, 8.

    Van der Marel & Anderson 2010: the fitted shallow cusp is not a central detection. Their global generalized-Nuker fit gives \(\Gamma=0.05\pm0.02\), a formal 1σ error. But fitting only \(R\leq15″\) gives [ \Gamma=0.00\pm0.07\quad(1\sigma). ] They interpret \(\Gamma\lesssim0.07\) as an approximately 1σ upper limit: the \(\Gamma=0.05\) model overpredicts both innermost points by about 1σ and is preferred because it fits better near 10″. Their spherical anisotropic Jeans analysis yields \(M_\bullet\lesssim1.2\times10^4\,M_\odot\) at approximately 1σ, or \(1.8\times10^4\,M_\odot\) at approximately 3σ, allowing core and shallow-cusp alternatives. van der Marel & Anderson 2010, §§4, 6–7.

    Noyola et al. 2010: renewed kinematic evidence, rather than an independent density-cusp measurement. VLT/FLAMES integrated spectra gave a central dispersion of \(22.8\pm1.2\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}}\)—the quoted measurement error—and a kinematic centre approximately 3.5″ from Anderson & van der Marel’s. Spherical isotropic fits produced BH masses of approximately \(3.0\)–\(5.2\times10^4\,M_\odot\) depending on centre; this is a range across centre choices, not a confidence interval. The paper’s \(\Delta\chi^2=1\) curves provide the formal one-parameter 1σ errors. Noyola et al. 2010, §§3–4.

    Modern centre and IMBH results¶

    Pechetti et al. 2024 find counter-rotation inside approximately 20″. Their preferred rotation centre is \(13^{\rm h}26^{\rm m}47.31^{\rm s},-47^\circ28′51.39″\), with approximately 1.5″ RA and 5″ Dec uncertainties, estimated from a joint 1σ \(\Delta\chi^2=2.3\) map. Other methods perform worse because the core is flat and the kinematics complex. This is not a precise replacement for the star-count centre. Their spectroscopy found no significant central high-velocity outliers, a result subsequently overtaken in depth by the HST proper-motion search. Pechetti et al. 2024, §§5–6.

    Häberle et al. 2024, Nature identify seven fast stars within 3″, with five forming the robust subset. Assuming they are bound and the background cluster escape speed is 62 km s\(^{-1}\), their combined projected positions and velocities require approximately \(8,200\,M_\odot\). This is a conditional dynamical minimum, not a stated 95% or 99% interval. Its minimum-mass location lies 0.3″ from the Anderson–van der Marel centre.

    Their acceleration-plus-velocity Bayesian analysis gives \(M_\bullet>21,100\,M_\odot\) at 99% confidence, with spatial priors and assumed bound orbits. These results do not establish a bright-star density cusp. Their 65.2% quality-cut pass fraction within 90″ is likewise not an artificial-star completeness measurement. Use the corrected coordinates and equation in the revised paper. Häberle et al. 2024, Methods; 2024 correction.

    Häberle et al. 2025, oMEGACat VI measure a central dispersion near 0.81 mas yr\(^{-1}\), approximately 21.1 km s\(^{-1}\) at their adopted distance. A smooth global Gaussian fit leaves a central dispersion excess inside 10″. Its fitted centre is offset by [ (\Delta{\rm RA},\Delta{\rm Dec})=(-1.31\pm0.72,\,+1.44\pm0.66)″ ] from the photometric centre. These are the quoted fit errors; §IV.2 does not explicitly state their confidence convention. The paper supports the same central vicinity but does not publish a new completeness-corrected number-density cusp. Häberle et al. 2025, §§III.2, IV.2.

    González Prieto, Rodriguez & Cabrera 2025 provide particularly relevant modelling. Their two highlighted evolutionary models reach 47,000 and 51,000 \(M_\odot\) after 12 Gyr; these are simulation outcomes, not observational confidence bounds. They reproduce the broad brightness and dispersion profiles, while predicting a slightly brighter inner core. Their stellar 3D mass-density slopes settle near \(\gamma\simeq1.3\), versus approximately 1.97–2.1 for stellar-mass BHs. The \(\gamma=1.75\) cusp elsewhere in their appendix belongs to an equal-mass benchmark, not their present-day omega Cen stellar population. González Prieto et al. 2025, §III.1 and Appendix A.

    Contradictory mass evidence remains. Bañares-Hernández et al. favour an extended \(2\)–\(3\times10^5\,M_\odot\) component with scale radius 1.5–2.2 pc, reporting these ranges at 3σ, and an IMBH upper bound of \(6,000\,M_\odot\) at 3σ. Their spherical Jeans model allows both extended remnants and a point mass. It uses older proper-motion data rather than jointly fitting the individual oMEGACat fast stars; its inferred background escape speed is approximately 10% higher. Thus its apparent conflict with the fast-star minimum involves different potentials and data, not merely incompatible formal error bars. Bañares-Hernández et al. 2025, §§3, 6, 8.3.

    I could not verify a relevant Evans et al. 2025/2026 central-density paper. The verified Evans, Strigari & Zivick study is 2022, based on axisymmetric kinematic modelling; it is not a new innermost star-count measurement. Evans et al. 2022.

    What density profile should an IMBH produce?¶

    Relaxed, equal-mass limit. The Bahcall–Wolf solution has [ f(\mathcal E)\propto\mathcal E^{1/4},\qquad n(r)\propto r^{-7/4}, ] requiring collisional relaxation in a BH-dominated potential. A pure extended power-law component projects to \(\Sigma\propto R^{-3/4}\). This is an equilibrium theoretical result, not a universal profile for every IMBH host. Bahcall & Wolf 1976.

    Multiple stellar masses. In the classical weak-segregation solution, lighter tracers approach \(r^{-3/2}\) while the heaviest component is steeper. Bright RGB/SGB stars are therefore not interchangeable with the total mass distribution, especially if stellar-mass BHs dominate central scattering. The precise slopes depend on the mass spectrum and component abundances. Bahcall & Wolf 1977.

    Finite-action DF: a separate route to \(r^{-3/2}\). Your model can generate this cusp without having reached Bahcall–Wolf equilibrium. If an isotropic DF tends to a finite, nonzero value \(f_0\) as the actions vanish, then in the Keplerian asymptotic regime [ n(r)\simeq\frac{4\pi}{3}f_0 \left(\frac{2GM_\bullet}{r}\right)^{3/2}. ] This is a phase-space-volume argument. “Finite” alone is insufficient: a DF tending to zero can behave differently. Collisionless adiabatic growth of a BH in an initially isothermal core gives the same \(r^{-3/2}\) asymptote. Quinlan, Hernquist & Sigurdsson 1995.

    A three-dimensional cusp can look nearly cored. Projection adds stars along the entire sightline through omega Cen’s much larger core. The asymptotic relation \(\Gamma=\gamma-1\) applies to a sufficiently extended pure cusp; it need not describe the total profile across a small transition region. Realistic cluster simulations already demonstrated shallow projected bright-star slopes of approximately −0.1 to −0.3, despite an IMBH and central mass segregation. Baumgardt, Makino & Hut 2005.

    Heating and scouring require a dynamical history. IMBH-driven energy generation can maintain a large cluster core. A hard BH binary can additionally eject central stars and destroy a cusp; subsequent relaxation can regrow it. Merritt & Szell’s simulations find regeneration over roughly one relaxation time, extending to approximately \(0.2r_{\rm infl}\). Those simulations concern galactic nuclei: they establish a mechanism, not evidence that omega Cen’s central bright stars were scoured. A stationary point mass by itself does not prescribe a deficit. Merritt & Szell 2006.

    Angular scales and the expected observable signal¶

    At \(D=5.4\) kpc, 1″ = 0.02618 pc. Using the explicit convention \(r_{\rm infl}=GM_\bullet/\sigma_{\rm 1D}^2\), with an illustrative \(\sigma_{\rm 1D}=22\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}}\), I obtain:

    \(M_\bullet\) \(r_{\rm infl}\) Angular radius
    \(8,200\,M_\odot\) 0.073 pc 2.8″
    \(20,000\,M_\odot\) 0.178 pc 6.8″
    \(40,000\,M_\odot\) 0.355 pc 13.6″
    \(50,000\,M_\odot\) 0.444 pc 17.0″

    These are calculated scales, not statistical constraints. The proposed 0.07–0.3 pc range is plausible for particular dispersions or influence-radius definitions, but its upper end is not universal.

    The observable cusp can occupy only a fraction of this radius. Consequently, a low-mass IMBH could have its strongest density signature mostly inside your bins, whereas a \(4\times10^4\,M_\odot\) model should be tested directly at 2–3″. Neither slope nor excess-star count follows from BH mass alone.

    Interpretation of your counts¶

    The following calculations use your supplied counts and predictions, without inspecting local data.

    Radius Observed \(4\times10^4\,M_\odot\) DF Observed/predicted No-BH DF Observed/predicted
    2.00–2.55″ 6 14.1 0.426 10.8 0.556
    2.55–3.24″ 12 22.1 0.543 17.5 0.686
    Combined 18 36.2 0.497 28.3 0.636

    The quoted probabilities are correctly lower-tail Poisson probabilities, \(P(N\leq N_{\rm obs}\mid\lambda)\): 0.01338 and 0.01438. For the no-BH predictions they are 0.0872 and 0.1116.

    Pooling the two bins gives \(P(N\leq18\mid36.2)=0.000643\), compared with 0.0266 for \(\lambda=28.3\). These are conditional tail probabilities for fixed predictions and a preselected region—not global rejection significances after fitting, choosing bins, varying the centre, or accounting for completeness uncertainty.

    Three implications follow.

    1. A 10–15% correction cannot by itself reconcile the bright counts. If missing stars explain everything, the BH model requires approximately 57% and 46% losses in those bins. Even the no-BH model requires approximately 44% and 31% losses. Thus the discrepancy is partly shared by both models.

    2. Flat magnitude ratios constrain differential completeness, not absolute completeness. They argue against a large additional loss confined to one bright slice. They permit a common spatial loss, centre-induced flattening, or a shared tracer deficit. If the reported ratios average broadly over \(R<10″\), the diagnostic is weaker still: your two annuli occupy only 6.5% of that area.

    3. The tension concerns the DF’s central occupation as well as \(M_\bullet\). A finite, nonzero small-action DF imposes a particular cusp and normalization. Rejecting that prediction would not automatically reject an IMBH with a different tracer DF, segregation history, or depleted orbit population.

    Removing quality cuts avoids one source of selection, but does not measure detection completeness or undo any requirements implicit in the parent catalogue’s construction.

    3. Disagreements, uncertainties and open questions¶

    What is established: a weak unresolved-light cusp was reported historically; completeness-corrected resolved counts instead support a core or very shallow cusp, with a marginal innermost depression. Modern kinematics provide substantial evidence for a compact central mass, while model-dependent mass estimates remain disputed.

    What is not established: a statistically secure, selectively bright-star deficit in omega Cen caused by saturation, collisions, tidal destruction, or BH-binary scouring. The published marginal depression has not acquired a verified physical attribution.

    For your specific discrepancy, the unresolved issues are:

    • Absolute local completeness: no verified oMEGACat recovery fraction presently justifies either assuming 100% or applying a factor-of-two correction at F625W \(<19\).
    • Centre uncertainty: approximately 1–2″ is consequential for these annuli. Predictions must be integrated around the same adopted centre as the data and marginalised over its uncertainty.
    • Spatial structure near bright stars: azimuthally averaged magnitude ratios can conceal small exclusion regions.
    • Tracer correspondence: the model must represent the exact magnitude-selected population, including its evolutionary phases and catalogue requirements.
    • DF flexibility: a physical IMBH test should allow alternatives to a nonzero constant small-action DF.

    My assessment: the evidence supports describing your result as a central bright-tracer shortfall relative to the fitted DF, with a published marginal precedent. Calling it documented crowding incompleteness, or documented dynamical scouring, would go beyond the sources.
